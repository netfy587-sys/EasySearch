Why bother

Three things a plant is actually doing for the room.

Not folklore — these are the mechanisms researchers have actually been able to measure indoors, at normal houseplant densities.

01

It scrubs specific pollutants

NASA's 1989 Clean Air Study found several common houseplants — pothos, spider plant, peace lily — measurably reduced benzene, formaldehyde and trichloroethylene in sealed test chambers over 24 hours.

02

It raises local humidity

Through transpiration, a single mature plant can release close to a litre of water vapour a week, gently lifting humidity in dry, heated or air-conditioned rooms.

03

It shifts attention, briefly

Studies on "micro-restoration" have linked even short glances at greenery to small, measurable drops in reported stress and mental fatigue during desk work.

Reading a room

Your window's compass direction matters more than the room itself.

A "bright" room can still be the wrong light for a plant, because what matters is which direction the window faces and how long direct sun actually reaches the leaves.

N

North-facing

The gentlest, most consistent light all day — no direct sun at all. Best for ferns, snake plants and other low-light natives.

E

East-facing

Soft direct sun for a few hours each morning, then indirect the rest of the day. A safe middle ground for most houseplants.

S

South-facing

The brightest exposure through most of the day. What succulents, cacti and citrus are actually asking for when they want "full sun."

W

West-facing

Intense, hot afternoon light. Can scorch thin-leafed plants unless filtered by a sheer curtain in peak summer months.

Sorted out

A few things everyone repeats about houseplants.

Some of this is half-true, some outdated, some just plant-shop folklore. Here's the actual state of things.

01
Widely repeated
You need dozens of plants to noticeably clean a room's air.
Closer to true
NASA's original study used sealed chambers far smaller than a real room; follow-up research suggests you'd need far more plants per square metre than most homes have to match a basic air filter.
02
Widely repeated
Yellow leaves always mean underwatering — give it more.
Closer to true
Yellowing is actually the classic sign of overwatered roots suffocating. Check soil moisture before adding more — it's the opposite instinct to the usual reflex.
03
Widely repeated
Talking to your plants helps them grow.
Closer to true
The voice itself likely does nothing, but the gentle airflow from consistent talking may slightly strengthen stems — similar to the effect of a small fan.
04
Widely repeated
Misting raises humidity enough to matter.
Closer to true
A spray bottle's effect evaporates within minutes. A pebble tray or humidifier changes the air around a plant far more meaningfully over a full day.

Most houseplants aren't fussy. They're just misread.

Match the light to what the species actually evolved for, water by the soil rather than the calendar, and the "difficult" reputation mostly disappears.
